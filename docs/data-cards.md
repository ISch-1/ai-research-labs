# Data cards

Every dataset used in the three labs: where it comes from, how it is used, its license as
stated by the source, and what to watch for. All datasets are **downloaded by code** into
`./data` on first use (never committed); plain-file sources are pinned by SHA-256.
Format inspired by Gebru et al. (2021), *Datasheets for datasets*, and Pushkarna et al. (2022),
*Data cards*. Licenses are summarised for convenience — the source's own terms prevail.

## Notes on selected datasets

### Adult

1994 US Census extract; the target is whether annual income exceeds $50K (23.9% positive). It
records sex and race and is heavily imbalanced in both (about two thirds male; most rows white);
many features (relationship, marital status, occupation) act as proxies for them. It is a
standard *fairness* benchmark precisely because naive models reproduce historical pay gaps.
Here it is used only to compare predictive performance and to demonstrate an API — no fairness
audit was done, and the served model must not inform decisions about people. Values are 30 years
old, in 1994 dollars.

### KDD Cup 1999

A simulated US Air Force network from 1998 (DARPA intrusion-detection evaluation). Its known
artefacts — duplicated records, attacks that are trivially separable, traffic statistics unlike
modern networks — make it a teaching dataset for anomaly detection, not a measure of real
intrusion-detection performance. Lab A uses the HTTP and SA subsets prepared by scikit-learn.

### Emotion

English tweets labelled with six emotions (sadness, joy, love, anger, fear, surprise) using
hashtags as distant supervision, so labels are noisy and depend on specific words — which is why
bag-of-words beats sentence embeddings on it (B1). Tweets can contain personal information and
offensive language; the dataset's terms restrict it to educational and research use.

### SQuAD v1.1

Crowd-sourced questions on Wikipedia paragraphs. Lab B turns the dev split into an open-domain
corpus (every paragraph is a passage) and holds out 6 whole articles to create questions the
corpus cannot answer. Questions were written while reading the paragraph, so they share its
words: this favours lexical retrieval (BM25) and short extractive answers.

### CIFAR-10

60,000 32×32 images in 10 classes, a labelled subset of the *80 Million Tiny Images*
collection, which was withdrawn in 2020 after offensive labels and images were found in it.
CIFAR-10's images were relabelled by hand into ten everyday classes, but the provenance is
worth knowing. C5's shortcut experiment *modifies* CIFAR-10 on the fly (a coloured corner
patch); nothing is redistributed.

### Oxford-IIIT Pet

7,349 photos of 37 breeds (12 cat, 25 dog), about 200 per breed, collected from the web and
annotated by the authors (Parkhi et al., 2012). Pure-bred animals in typical pet photos:
mixed breeds, other species and unusual viewpoints are absent, which limits what a model trained
on it can be used for. Some photos include owners. Lab C resizes images to 160 px and keeps a
stratified 10% of the trainval split for validation. Six resized test images are redistributed
with the demo under the same CC BY-SA 4.0 license, with attribution.
